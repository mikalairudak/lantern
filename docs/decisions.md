# Decisions

This log explains the decisions behind Lantern that a reader of the code would want explained. Each entry is headed by the date of the decision and the decision in one sentence, then says what is true now (**Decision**), the evidence or reason for it, with the numbers that settled it (**Why**), and what it gives up (**Cost**); where a decision changed, a short **How it got here** pairs what was tried with what retired it. One person, the owner, made these decisions: options were proposed, built and measured with AI coding agents, and the owner chose among them, so entries name no agent and no working session. Measurements beyond the one or two that justify a decision live in [`docs/harness.md`](harness.md) and in the specs that enforce them. This is an edited selection of about forty-five decisions from a longer working log.

### Glossary

- **Stop**: one place in the day at one time.
- **Version**: one of the places a stop could be: the place the model planned, or one of its two alternatives, one quieter and one livelier, within a 10-minute walk.
- **Energy**: how lively a place is, on five named levels: reflective, calm, balanced, lively, social.
- **The chart**: the day drawn with time running down and energy across. Each stop is a row: a horizontal wire with a bead for each version, the version shown filled and named beside it. The code calls it the abacus.
- **The curve**: the line drawn through the chart that says how lively the visitor wants each hour. Dragging it shows, at each stop, the version nearest to it.
- **The request sentence**: the page's headline, such as "A calm Saturday in Kyoto, with a partner". Every word is a control, and a word with a dotted underline is one Lantern assumed.
- **The intake**: the model call that reads the visitor's free text into a typed request.
- **A written day**: a day the live model wrote in a recorded run, played back from a file with no model call. The public site offers six.
- **A live day**: a day the model writes for a visitor's request while they watch.
- **A kept day**: a live day stored on the server and opened again at its own address for 30 days.
- **The shadow**: the same request written in the background by a cheaper model, never shown, kept for comparison.
- **The fork**: how a changed word is answered: the model proposes a new version of the part of the day that word shaped, drawn beside the day for the visitor to take or leave.
- **The open stop**: a stop's details, opened over the chart from its name.
- **The studio**: the password-protected part of the site, holding the lab, where each component is drawn across its states, and the project's story.

## 2026-10-20 — Two-layer data contract

**Decision.** The schema has two layers (`src/lib/schema/itinerary.schema.ts`). The generation layer, the `Generated*` schemas, is what the model is constrained to write: content only. The app layer (`Itinerary`, `Stop`, `Place`) is what components render: the same content plus ids, the request's city, date and time zone, and invariants checked across the whole day. An app-layer day has no blank or invisible-only strings, no stop that starts before the previous one ends, and no stop longer than eight hours. A normalizer (`normalizeDay`) builds the app layer from a finished generation. Places and the day's header derive from the generation layer with `.extend()` and `.pick()`; stops are reshaped by hand, since a generated stop's place becomes a primary place plus its alternatives.

**Why.** The model should own content, not ids, request context or invariants. A schema the provider enforces cannot express "starts after the previous stop" anyway, and a schema strict enough to try would turn a small slip into a failed day: `"8:00"` instead of `"08:00"`, or a stop that runs five minutes into the next, should be repaired, not thrown away with the other seven stops. Two layers also draw one clear line of responsibility. If the model's output fails the generation layer, the model slipped, and the normalizer decides what to repair or refuse. If a normalized day fails the app layer, the bug is ours. Components never meet a day that broke either rule, so they are designed for the schema's values rather than its failures. Checking invariants across the whole day, rather than field by field, avoids an app-layer copy of every shape the layers share, which is the drift two layers invite.

**Cost.** Two schemas to keep aligned, and the hand-made reshaping of stops is where drift can creep in. A long visit, such as a hike, has to be split into stops of eight hours or less. The normalizer has real work to do before the app layer sees anything ("The normalizer repairs slips and refuses what it cannot make whole").

## 2026-10-20 — A provider-portable generation schema

**Decision.** The generation layer uses no numeric, length, count, pattern or format keywords, every object is strict, and every field is required, never optional. A field the model may leave empty says so with an empty string or an enum member such as `"none"`. The one exception is a day's `minItems: 1` on its stops ("A day has at least one stop"). A test walks the emitted JSON Schema and fails on any other keyword. Limits the model must respect, such as a name's length or a swap's walking distance, are written into the field descriptions and enforced by the normalizer.

**Why.** Providers disagree on what structured outputs can enforce. Anthropic's rejects `minimum`, `maxLength` and `minItems` above 1, for example, and Zod 4 quietly emits integer bounds for `.int()` that a provider may refuse. A schema written to the smallest common subset keeps the choice of provider open and keeps the contract readable as plain JSON Schema. It also separates two kinds of rule cleanly. The schema says what shape the model must produce, which the provider can guarantee while it decodes. The normalizer says which values the app will accept, which only code can check. A limit in a description is a request to the model; a limit in the normalizer is a guarantee to the components. Keeping those apart means a provider's quirks never decide what a visitor sees.

**Cost.** Limits are soft at generation time: the model can write a 40-character name or an option 1.3 km away, and only the normalizer catches it, as a counted repair. Each limit lives in two places, its description and its check, so a change touches both. A new union type or optional field is not portable by default and waits for a live probe ("The model's schema keeps its union types few").

## 2026-10-20 — Field order is render order

**Decision.** Fields are ordered the way they appear on screen. A day writes its assumptions first (the request sentence's words), then its one line about the day (`dek`), then its stops, then the swap options as a trailing list linked to stops by `stopIndex`, never nested inside each stop. Inside a place, everything its row shows comes first: name, kind, neighbourhood, coordinates, sensory profile with its energy, and light. The summary and tags, which only the open stop shows, come last. Every simulated stream writes its fixture in the schema's order (`inSchemaOrder`), so the lab and the tests see fields arrive as the model writes them.

**Why.** A stream paints from the top. Nesting two alternatives inside every stop would hold back each next stop behind about two extra places' worth of tokens, so the timeline would fill at about a third of its pace. Inside a place, a row cannot be laid out until it knows its energy, which decides how much it says, and its best light, which may add a sun beside its name. In the original order a place's light arrived about 1.48 s after its name on Sonnet's recorded days; with the row's fields ahead of the summary it arrives after 0.84 s. Moving whole blocks kept the shape, so every recorded output still parses and no stored day needed migrating.

**Cost.** Alternatives arrive after the whole timeline, so swap controls appear late in the stream. The model now chooses a place's energy and light before it writes why the place earns its slot, which could make the summary repeat the light note; the day rounds read for it. A row still waits a beat, under a second at a live model's pace, for its energy and light before it shows.

**How it got here.** The day's order was set at the start; the place's order changed later, once rows were made to wait until everything they show is known rather than reflow as fields arrive, and the wait for the light was measured.

## 2026-10-20 — Swaps change selection, not data

**Decision.** A stop keeps its primary place, up to two alternatives and a `selectedPlaceId`. Ids are positions in the generation (`stop-0`, `stop-0:p`, `stop-0:a1`), unique per itinerary, and double as Motion `layoutId`s. `selectedPlaceId` is the day's plan: the model's pick, or the version a changed energy word moves it to. A swap never writes it. The visitor's picks are a separate map from stop id to place id, held beside the day in `useEnergyCurve` and kept on this device (`src/features/day/picked-places.ts`). Resetting goes back to the plan. No place's data ever changes.

**Why.** Swapping back is free, nothing is mutated, and streaming keys stay identical after the stream completes, so nothing remounts when the finished day replaces the draft. Keeping picks apart from the plan means three things can be told apart at any moment: what the model planned, what the visitor chose and what the page shows. Two features depend on that. The line about the day was written around the plan, so the page can say when the visitor has moved away from it, leading the line with "As first planned:". And a re-plan sends the picks as ids, which the server checks against its own record of the day, so the model is told what the visitor sees without the client sending any text about places.

**Cost.** The schema checks id uniqueness and a valid selection with refinements. Ids are positions, so a dropped stop leaves a gap in the numbering, and a re-plan that keeps a stop must keep its position. Picks kept on one device do not follow the day's link to another.

## 2026-10-20 — The model describes light; code computes it

**Decision.** The model supplies each place's exposure (open, dappled, covered or interior), the direction its view faces, the window when its light is best, and a one-line note. The light at a stop's scheduled time comes from the sun's altitude, computed from the place's coordinates, the date and the city's time zone (`src/lib/geo/light-windows.ts`). The windows, sunrise, sunset and the golden hours, are computed at each place's own coordinates. A stop gets a small sun beside its name only when the model said its place is best at sunrise or sunset and the visit spends at least 20 minutes inside that computed window; the light-sense quality check reads the same window through the same helper.

**Why.** Sun position is deterministic, so a model should never be asked to guess it, and a model states an invented time as confidently as a true one. What the model is good at is the qualitative part: that a terrace faces west, that a moss garden is best before the sun clears the trees. Splitting the two means the chart and the quality check can never disagree about the same stop, which an earlier rule allowed: it marked any open-air stop that spent 20 minutes in a golden hour, whatever the model had said about the place. The sun speaks only about the visitor's own hour. A "best at sunset" on a half-past-eight visit would read as a mistake the day made, so a visit outside its window gets no mark rather than a hint.

**Cost.** The itinerary must carry a date and an IANA time zone such as `Asia/Tokyo`; offsets, `UTC` and abbreviations like `EST` are rejected, though `Intl` accepts them, because they carry no daylight-saving rules for a place. Weather is unknown, so every light is computed for a clear sky. The day never says that a place has a better hour than the one planned.

## 2026-10-20 — Designing for Dynamic UI

**Decision.** The model writes the content, so a component is designed for the whole space of data and states the schema allows, not for one mockup: the shortest and longest text, every empty field, zero to two alternatives; every lifecycle state from nothing yet to interactive, plus every ending without a day; and open, standard and folded densities. Nothing is drawn before it arrives, so there are no skeletons: when the stream falls silent for 0.4 s, the place where the next row will land names the part of the day being written ("the afternoon"), and a line under the chart counts the stops written so far. Each component lists its states, densities and data extremes as plain data in a `labMatrix` entry beside it (`src/lib/lab-matrix.ts`); one renderer in the lab draws every cell in both themes, at a phone's width and a laptop's, and `pnpm e2e:visual` shoots them against a pixel baseline. A stream scrubber plays any fixture day frame by frame and ends it in each outcome.

**Why.** A component polished for one happy-path mockup looks accidental with real generated data and partial streams: a name that stops mid-word, a duration that grows from 1 to 15 to 150 as it streams, a day cut inside its swaps. A list of states is something a person can read, diff and extend, where a page of hand-placed examples drifts. Themes and widths belong to the renderer, so no component can leave out dark mode or the phone. The scrubber makes the stream's hardest moments reachable on purpose. Phone cells are CSS containers, so their type reads true at any viewport width: before that, a phone cell in a laptop's viewport set a laptop's type, and neither the shots nor a person at a desk saw the phone's header.

**Cost.** More states per component, and the matrices and their baseline take upkeep: about a thousand shots, past the baseline's size budget in git. Every intended design change needs a look at its diff and a deliberate update. The scrubber's frames are parsed from text prefixes, so a frame may be one a live client never sees. A matrix's data must live in a plain module, since a client module's exports are only references on the server.

## 2026-10-21 — Alternatives are a quieter and a livelier version

**Decision.** A stop's alternatives are versions of the same kind of stop nearby: one quieter and one livelier, within a 10-minute walk (800 m) and open at the same time. A stop at the quietest or liveliest level gets only the one toward the middle. Energy, sound and crowd are each five named levels rather than scores; energy runs reflective, calm, balanced, lively, social. Energy is independent of sound and crowd, since a silent communal table can still be social. The normalizer checks the versions and drops one past the walk.

**Why.** An earlier draft offered two alternatives with the same feel as the original, a plain swap. Making them quieter and livelier turned the swap and the energy control into one system: with up to three versions per stop on an energy axis, the energy the visitor wants can pick, for each stop, the version nearest to it, locally and instantly, with no model call. The model writes the options; code chooses among them. Named levels were chosen over 0–100 scores because models answer named levels more consistently, and five steps give the chart five lanes. A level is also the natural unit of change: the curve and the arrow keys move by levels, and the layout is recomputed once per step rather than on every pixel of a drag.

**Cost.** Every day needs two more places per stop, each another chance for the model to name a place that is wrong or too far. Named levels are coarser than a score. A stop at either end of the scale has one version, so the curve can do nothing for it in one direction, and a day that never goes quiet leaves its quietest lane empty.

## 2026-10-21 — The day is drawn as an abacus

**Decision.** The day is a chart (`src/features/abacus/`, called the abacus in the code). Time runs down over waking hours, to scale but never closer than a row needs; energy runs across in five lanes, from reflective to social. Each stop is one row: a horizontal wire at its time, and on it a bead for every version at its energy. The version shown is a filled bead with its name, kind and quarter written beside it; the others are hollow. How much a row says follows the energy of the version it shows: a calm or reflective place adds a line on how it sounds and feels, a lively or social one says how full and loud it is in two words ("busy · chatter"). Tapping a hollow bead swaps to it; tapping a name opens the stop's details over the chart. Every bead is a 24 px pointer target and a named control for a screen reader.

**Why.** It was picked from four layouts drawn on the same day, at rest and mid-drag. It is the only one where the whole option space the model wrote is visible at once, and where the curve is seen choosing from it: the model writes the options, code picks. Lanes with tick marks hid the alternatives until a stop was selected, and a river-like ribbon came out noisy. Writing each stop's words at its bead, rather than in a list beside the chart, made the layout itself say how lively each place is, and on a phone it brought seven of eight names above the fold, where there had been a column of unnamed beads. Density by energy gives a drag a visible effect: pulling lunch livelier folds its row, and the rows below ease up. A first rule folded lively rows to their name alone, and the owner found they looked as if their data were missing, so every row now has a second line.

**Cost.** Names are ragged on the left, so finding a place by name is slower than in a column; the list view covers that ("A day can be read as a list, written as the chart is"). Words sit over the chart, so the curve fades under each line of words. A swap can change a row's height and move the rows below it. Two versions at one energy stand a target's width apart, so one of them is drawn slightly off its true energy.

## 2026-10-21 — The request sentence is the input

**Decision.** The request is one sentence, and it is the page's headline: "A calm Saturday in Kyoto, on foot, with a partner." Each word is a control that opens its options in place; there is no form and no chat box. Besides the visitor's own words, the sentence shows what Lantern assumed, with a dotted underline, and may end with one open question as an empty word (", with …?"). The words are typed in the contract: each has a closed vocabulary (city, date and energy, then travel, start, dinner, end and company), the model writes its assumptions first, and each stop lists the words it was planned around (`dependsOn`). Whether a word was chosen, assumed or asked is worked out by the normalizer from the request, never labelled by the model. The model writes one line about this day, not a title.

**Why.** A visitor should refine the day with taps and see every answer reshape it. Answer tiles read as a booking site's filters, and a question before any answer reads as a chat. Visible assumptions make the model's reasoning editable: everything it filled in is on screen and one tap from being changed. Only assumptions are marked, because a first version also underlined the visitor's own words, seven of the sentence's nine phrases, and a first-time reader took them for links that did nothing. The source of each word is computed rather than generated because a model asked to label its own words could call a word assumed that the visitor chose; null read against the request says it exactly. `dependsOn` is what later let a changed word re-plan only the part of the day it shaped.

**Cost.** Every word needs a designed edit state, and a new answer, such as a bike or a late start, is a contract change. The model may leave more than one word open, and a hard check fails such a day. The visitor's words carry no underline, so they need their own hover and focus marks to look changeable.

## 2026-10-21 — Tokens are enforced, not documented

**Decision.** Every visual value is a token. The Tailwind theme is closed (`--*: initial` in `src/app/globals.css`), so an off-token colour, size, space, width or radius has no utility at all. Lint turns an unknown class into an error and rejects arbitrary values, per-class durations, `dark:` overrides, removed focus outlines, raw colours, literal values in `style` and CSS imports. Colour tokens are `light-dark()` pairs declared inline, so `data-theme` on any element switches its subtree. A palette test (`src/lib/tokens/palette.test.ts`) reads the stylesheet and holds the bars: text at 5:1 contrast on every surface, drawn marks at 3:1, and the accents for light, energy and swaps at least 12 ΔE2000 apart as protan and deutan eyes see them. Other tests pin the lint rules and the match between the stylesheet and the token catalogue shown in the lab.

**Why.** A closed theme alone fails silently: a class outside it simply renders unstyled. Lint makes the wrong thing loud, which outlasts any written rule, especially when much of the code is written by coding agents. Colours resolve per element because a `--color-*` variable on `:root` resolves once there, so every subtree would silently take the page's theme. The palette test exists because pixel baselines missed what mattered: at Playwright's default threshold, a whole new day ground passed as unchanged. An early palette also put the sun's amber and the curve's olive only 4 to 8 ΔE2000 apart for protan and deutan vision, about one man in twelve, so the sun and the curve looked alike to them; the test now fails that.

**Cost.** Every new value needs a token first, listed in `src/lib/tokens/catalog.ts`. Lint only finds class strings where it is told to look, so class strings must live there. Utilities that need no theme, such as opacity and z-index, stay open and are left to review. No `--color-*` variable exists at runtime, so SVG and Motion take colour through utilities.

## 2026-10-22 — The normalizer repairs slips and refuses what it cannot make whole

**Decision.** `normalizeDay(request, day)` in `src/lib/normalizer/normalize.ts` returns the itinerary with every repair it made, or a refusal with its reason. It repairs text with spaces or invisible characters at its ends, a start time like `"7:30"`, a duration that is not whole or runs past eight hours, a stop that runs into the next (cut at the next start), too many tags or options, options in the wrong order, an option past the 800 m walk, and an option spelled as one of the day's own stops. It drops a stop it cannot show: no valid time, no stay, blank text, coordinates off the map, or a start no later than the stop before it, read on a clock that runs past midnight. It refuses a day whose line is blank or that has no stop left.

**Why.** The app layer's invariants then hold for whatever the model wrote, so components never meet a broken day. Dropping one bad stop beats failing a whole day for it, and a day with nothing left to show is not a day. Every repair is named and counted, so a prompt that drifts shows up in the eval as a rising repair count before a visitor notices. Two rules came from recorded days. A lively Lisbon or Madrid day ends with a bar at half past midnight, which the normalizer first dropped as out of order, so stop times stay clock strings ("00:15") and are read on a running clock (`runningSpans` in `src/lib/time/clock.ts`). And on recorded rounds Sonnet offered one of its own day's stops as a swap option in 6 of 81 days, Haiku in 32 of 82, which could put the same place in the day twice.

**Cost.** A dropped stop leaves a gap the model did not plan, visible only in the repair count. The past-midnight rule has thresholds (a start before 06:00 after a stop that ended at 21:00 or later), so a night that pauses earlier is cut where the rule ends. A stop that loses an option has nothing for the curve to pick on that side.

## 2026-10-22 — The stream reports how it ended

**Decision.** Generation streams through AI SDK 7: `streamText` with `Output.object` on the server, sent as the SDK's UI message stream. Every stream ends with one explicit outcome (`src/lib/stream/outcome.ts`): complete, cut, disconnected, refused, invalid, unavailable or limited. One reader of our own, `readDayStream` in `src/lib/stream/read-day-stream.ts`, turns any stream, live, recorded or simulated, into partial days and that outcome, and applies the salvage rule once: a stream cut inside the swap options shows the day without the missing swaps, one cut inside the stops shows no partial day, and a refusal never shows one. The server reads the same bytes through a tee with the same reader, so the log records what the visitor's reader made of them. Each ending without a day says what happened in one sentence and offers one way on, in the header's place (`src/features/day/stream-ending.ts`).

**Why.** With the SDK's object hook and a plain text stream, every failure reached the browser as HTTP 200: a refusal, a token limit, an error mid-stream and exhausted retries all looked like a stream that simply ended. Since the portable schema sets no counts, a day cut between its swap options even parsed as complete. This was reproduced on the SDK's mock model before any live call. Without an outcome the page cannot tell a finished day from a broken one. The salvage rule follows from field order: a cut always leaves the beginning of the day, swaps only enrich a complete timeline, and a timeline missing its afternoon is a broken day. Claude's documentation advises discarding partial output after a refusal. The endings' words follow what the visitor can do next: a refusal is not offered again, since the same request would most likely be refused again.

**Cost.** The reader uses two helpers that `ai` exports but does not document, so `ai` is pinned to an exact version, with a short parser of our own as the fallback. A stop whose swaps were cut has no swap control, so zero alternatives has to look intentional. Tests replay fixture days through the SDK's own mock model (`src/lib/stream/simulate-day-stream.ts`), which injects each failure and goes through the same repair and parsing as a live stream; a dropped connection needed a small custom stream of its own.

## 2026-10-22 — The energy curve stays on the client and picks among the versions the model wrote

**Decision.** The request carries one energy word for the whole day; the curve the visitor drags is local state over the day the model wrote (`src/features/abacus/curve.ts`, `src/features/abacus/use-energy-curve.ts`). Dragging bends one curve through the stops' energies, and each stop shows the version nearest to it, with no model call. The curve is drawn with monotone slopes (Steffen's method), so between two stops it never swings past either. A stop switches version only when another is nearer the curve by a fifth of a level (`SWITCH_MARGIN`). A pull moves only the stops on either side of the pointer, each by how near it was grabbed, and a pull at a stop's own wire moves that stop alone. The chart follows the curve during a drag; the headline and the list change once, on release. A press that moves less than 4 px is a tap.

**Why.** Sending the curve to the model would make every drag a generation, paid and slow. A word like "a calm Saturday" gives the model enough to shape the day, and the curve then moves along that shape for free. Monotone slopes keep two lively stops in a row lively between them. Without the margin, a pointer resting between two beads makes the shown version flicker. The local pull came from the owner's own use: dragging a stop quieter on the live Kyoto day, they saw the stops above and below follow and read it as a bug. The curve had bent like a rubber band, reaching 80 minutes either side with four fifths of the pull, and many of Kyoto's stops are about an hour apart. A test over every stop of that day, dragged both ways, now holds that no other stop swaps.

**Cost.** A bead switches a fifth of a level late in either direction, so the same curve can show different versions depending on the way it came. Monotone slopes flatten the curve at peaks and troughs. During a drag the chart and the rest of the page disagree; the chart marks each stop's committed version with a faint ghost bead. A visitor who wants the whole day quieter changes the energy word, which re-picks every stop at once.

CI runs the same, and the production build check, on every push to `develop` or `main` and every pull request, and `pnpm e2e` on pull requests, on pushes to `main` and by hand ("CI runs the e2e in four shards, skips it for inert paths, and runs the frame tests apart"). `pnpm build:check` (`scripts/build-check.mts`) builds production, serves it, and asks it for the pages a visitor or a crawler opens, a kept day's address with a fresh random id among them, failing on any 5xx or logged error. `E2E_CPU_RATE` slows every test page's CPU through Chrome's own throttling (`e2e/fixtures.ts`); `pnpm e2e:busy` runs the suite at half speed, and a frame-by-frame spec runs that way, repeated eight times, before it lands. Tests never call a live service: they run on the AI SDK's mock model and on recorded responses.

## 2026-10-23 — A row's words go where its bead comes to rest, and rows ease rather than jump

**Decision.** The motion rule is that what a visitor is reading never jumps. A written row moves at most about 3 px a frame (`MAX_ROW_PX_PER_FRAME` in `e2e/day-still.spec.ts`), so rows that grow, open or swap ease the rows below on one spring. A row's words are laid out where its bead will come to rest, measured on an unseen copy before they show, and its space is held from the moment its energy arrives, so a row only ever grows. A chart frame, a date line or an axis is drawn only once its place is known, rather than drawn early and slid into place. A gesture over the whole day, such as a new energy word, is the one exception: its rows set off together and settle in about 1.5 s. Under reduced motion a row lands whole once everything it shows has arrived. Every spring comes from `SPRINGS` in `src/lib/tokens/motion.ts`.

**Why.** Sampled frame by frame on a 390 px phone, the first streamed day moved rows already written by up to 55 px: a name followed its sliding bead, flipped sides and rewrapped, and its row's height swung the rows below. After these rules the worst move of a written row was 1.7 px. On a page that a model writes for 40 to 50 seconds, layout that shifts under the eye reads as a broken page, and a visitor cannot read what will not hold still; about 3 px a frame is slow enough to follow. A gesture is different: the visitor caused it and expects the answer quickly, and holding a whole day's change to 3 px a frame made one settle in 5.3 s.

**Cost.** The day appears later than the stream allows: a row waits for its energy before it shows, up to about two seconds at a live model's pace, and the header settles before the first row lands. Each row measures its words on every render, which is cheap at a day's twelve rows. ## 2026-10-28 — Places are checked through Geoapify, not Google Places

**Decision.** Every place a live day names is looked up in Geoapify, which serves OpenStreetMap's data. A named place is confirmed only when three things agree within 1 km of the model's point: its distance fits its kind, its name matches (ignoring diacritics, word order, common words and its own district and city), and its category fits its kind. A place to eat or drink is an area, so it is confirmed by counting places of its kind within a three-minute walk, 240 m: three or more confirm it (`src/lib/places/area.ts`). A failed lookup or a spent quota leaves a place unconfirmed; the day is never lost. A confirmed place carries a quiet map mark after its quarter. Nothing on the day says a place is not confirmed; the open stop says how far the map vouches for it.

**Why.** The owner's condition for going live was that nothing could run up a bill. Google Places needs a card on file, its key cannot be restricted to an address on Vercel, and it has no monthly cap; Geoapify's free plan needs no card. Measured on the 66 places of the first three written days, the right place came back for about 54. Distance and name alone confirmed 46, two of them wrong; adding the category confirmed 39, one wrong. Two simpler rules failed outright: a soft location bias returned Toronto, and wrong places came back with the provider's top confidence score, so the score is not used. Confirmation is shown quietly, and on confirmed places only, because the rule's promise is narrow: a place of this name and kind is at this spot, wrong about once in 39.

**Cost.** In Kyoto about half the places stay unconfirmed, some of them right, rather than widen the radius and confirm wrong neighbours. The provider's limit of five requests a second is queued per process, so several serverless instances together can exceed it. OpenStreetMap and Geoapify are credited on every day's page, as their terms ask. Opening hours are never checked, and the open stop says so where a place is found. Google stays the fallback if too many places stay unconfirmed.

## 2026-10-28 — Every model call holds its worst case against the spend caps before it is made

**Decision.** Live generation runs only behind `LIVE_GENERATION=1`, on in production since 31 October 2026. Before any model call, the route takes the visitor's count (10 days and 20 intake reads per visitor per UTC day, `VISITOR_DAILY_LIMITS`), then holds the call's worst case against the app's caps ($10 a day and $95 a calendar month, `APP_SPEND_CAPS` in `src/lib/live/limits.ts`). The worst case is the call's input ceiling read and its output cap written: about $0.14 for a Sonnet day held to 12,000 output tokens (`DAY_MAX_OUTPUT_TOKENS`). Over either limit, the answer is a 429, before any call, which the page shows as `limited`. When the call ends, the hold is settled with the cost the provider reported.

**Why.** Holding the worst case is what makes "the app cannot spend past its cap" true while calls run at once; counting only finished calls would let a burst through, one call for each request in flight. The visitor's count comes first, so a visitor past their limit holds none of the cap, however many requests they send at once. The monthly cap sits under the provider workspace's $100 limit, so a month ends in Lantern's own `limited`, which still offers the written days, rather than in the provider refusing every call. The output cap came from measurement: a day's JSON runs at about 2.4 characters a token, not the four first assumed, so a lively Kyoto day wrote 6,439 tokens and would have been cut by the first, lower cap. At 12,000 a day has nearly twice the room it uses and takes about 85 s at worst, inside the route's 150 s.

**Cost.** The hold is far above a typical day's $0.08, so the daily cap admits about 70 days with their shadows at once; settled at what they cost, it still affords about 120 a day. A call that ends with no usage reported is charged its whole hold, so the cap counts high on bad days. A runaway day can cost a few cents more than under the lower cap.

## 2026-10-28 — The limits and the log live in Postgres, and every hold expires

**Decision.** The visitors' counts, the app's spend and the generation log live in Postgres on Neon (`db/migrations/`, `src/lib/live/postgres-stores.ts`). Money is kept in whole micro-dollars. A visitor's take is one upsert that increments only below the limit. A call's worst case is held as its own row that expires ten minutes later, past any route's longest run. A reservation locks the day's and the month's rows in a fixed order and admits the call only if spent money, unexpired holds and the new hold stay within both caps; a settle deletes the hold and adds the real cost. The routes fail closed: a database that is down, slower than one shared 8 s deadline, or not migrated answers 503 and calls no model, and anything taken is given back.

**Why.** In memory, every serverless instance kept its own counters, so the caps multiplied by the number of instances. A hold kept as a running sum could be stranded for the rest of the month by a failed log write, a lost answer or a timeout; a hold that expires cannot. Spent money and caps are checked in one locked transaction because a read followed by a write lets concurrent calls through: on a real Postgres with 20 connections at once, removing the lock let 14 calls past a monthly cap that admits 3. The intake's model read needs nothing from the database, so when a process has not heard from the database for four minutes it starts the read beside the limits' check: Neon's free plan sleeps after five idle minutes, and waking it was 1.2 s of a 4.1 s cold intake.

**Cost.** About five round trips to the database before a day's first words. A take whose answer is lost after Neon committed it costs that visitor one day. An intake read started early and then refused by the limits is still paid; a process starts at most one every four minutes, so this costs at most about $0.33 a day per process, charged to the app's spend. Unit tests run on PGlite, one connection at a time, which proves each check is one statement but nothing about locking; an opt-in test runs the stores on a real Postgres.

## 2026-10-30 — The model's schema keeps its union types few

**Decision.** To Anthropic's structured outputs a nullable field is a union type, and union types weigh most in the grammar the provider compiles a schema into. So the generated schemas say "none" without null: a place's light direction and best window take an enum member, `"none"` (`NONE`), which the normalizer and the stream's drafts read as null (`noneAsNull`). The day's schema keeps five union types, the request's open words in `assumptions`; the re-plan's has none and the intake's nine. A test lists every union in every generated schema. No union type or optional field joins a generated schema until the live schema probe (`EVAL_ROUND=schema pnpm eval:live`, `src/eval/schema-probe.ts`) shows the provider accepts it.

**Why.** One live pass sent 24 day calls and the provider refused every one with a 400, "The compiled grammar is too large", at no cost. The only structural change since the last schema that ran was one new nullable field on a place, which repeats in two lists: nine union types became eleven. Anthropic's documentation allows at most 16 union types in a request and calls them especially expensive to compile, with an unstated limit on grammar size below that, which this hit. Descriptions are not part of the grammar, so the limit could not be seen from field counts or tested offline. Taking the light's four nullable fields out left room for what the next contract change needed.

**Cost.** A generated place says a missing value differently from the app, in one word read in one place. About 120 lines of fixtures changed mechanically. The limit cannot be measured offline: the probe asks the provider before any round, free when the provider's token count compiles the grammar too, and otherwise one call held to a few output tokens, about $0.015.

## 2026-10-30 — A day has at least one stop

**Decision.** `GeneratedDaySchema` requires at least one stop, `minItems: 1`, the one keyword outside the portable set, which Anthropic's structured outputs support; the portability test names it as the exception. Claude's decoder does not enforce it in practice: `@ai-sdk/anthropic` strips every count from the schema it sends and writes "min items: 1." into the field's description instead, which a test pins. So an empty day is refused by the stream's reader, which parses through the schema. The empty attempt costs the visitor nothing: the route gives back their count (`giveBackEmptyDay`), and the page holds its last frame and asks once more before it says the day could not be finished.

**Why.** In the second live pass Sonnet once wrote a day's assumptions and its line about the day, then closed the stops empty: 128 tokens, a day the visitor's limit paid for and the page could only call unfinished. A keyword the decoder ignores still tells the model in words, and the reader is the one place every stream passes, live or recorded. The owner set the refund and the single retry narrowly, for this failure alone, because the visitor did not cause it and it was the failure actually seen. Other invalid days, such as a blank line or stops the normalizer cannot show, stay counted, since refunding those would make every such failure a free second call.

**Cost.** A provider without `minItems` would accept an empty day, and only the reader would catch it. The close of every live day's stream waits for the server's own reading of it, under a millisecond on a warm process and up to the stores' deadline when the database is slow. An empty day is paid for twice at the provider. While the day is asked again, the empty day's line about the day stands for a second over the new day's first stops.

## 2026-10-30 — Lantern names no restaurant, café or bar

**Decision.** A stop of kind `coffee`, `food` or `bar` names an area, never one establishment: what is eaten or drunk there and where, in five words or fewer, such as "Petiscos on Rua das Flores" or "Obanzai along Pontocho". A dish leads a name only where nearly every place there serves it. It is confirmed by counting places of its kind around its point, not by looking up a name. Sights, walks and markets keep their own names and their check by name. The naming rules are measured as a share of each day's meal names (`meal-name`) and never fail a day.

**Why.** Of 408 places Sonnet named in the first two live passes, 29 were invented, and all 29 were cafés, restaurants or bars; the name check also confirmed 13 places on a neighbour of the same name, most of them restaurants. Dropping every unconfirmed food place would have removed the 29 at the cost of 58 real ones, nearly two stops a day. The owner decided against naming venues at all: naming a business also prefers it over its neighbours, which Lantern cannot stand behind, and a named place brings its opening hours and closures with it. A quarter known for eating is true for years, and whether it has places to eat is a question the map answers well. The naming check began as a hard check and then failed 29 of 36 days on both models in one round; a check that every day fails says nothing, so it became a share that moves when a prompt change helps.

**Cost.** A meal is less exact: the visitor picks the door on the spot. The meal word comes from the hour alone, so ice cream at sunset says "dinner". The venue checks read words, not meaning: a one-word venue inside a name is missed, and a line that describes one venue without naming it is caught by no check, only by a reader. The models still break the length rule often; about half of Sonnet's meal names on the latest round fit in two lines on a phone.

## 2026-10-30 — A day for a city Lantern has not written carries the intake's signature

**Why.** The city is the one free text the day's model reads, and the zone decides the date and the light. The intake already reads the visitor's text under its own limit and resolves the city through Geoapify, so what it signs is the map provider's name and zone, never the visitor's words. Signing only what the day route reads keeps the contract honest: whichever route first reads another field widens the signature with it. Deriving the key from an existing secret needs no new one, and the label keeps the salt from signing anything else.

**Cost.** A signature never expires and is bound to no visitor: one resolved city can be replayed for any number of days, each still counted against that visitor's ten and the app's caps. **Cost.** "Opened again" counts within a UTC day only: a visitor back the next day is a new visitor. ## 2026-11-06 — A paid eval round runs the routes' own code in its own process

## 2026-11-11 — A city's written day is one of its seasons, picked by the date it is shown on

**Decision.** A written day stays in the repository as code, and a city can hold more than one, each a season with a range of dates, `from` and `through` (`WrittenSeason` in `src/lib/days/written/seasons.ts`, listed in `start-days.ts`). The date a page shows (`shownDateOf`) picks the season (`seasonOn`); the clock never does. So the page, its line, its version and its place checks name one season, the route plays the season of the date it is asked for, and a re-plan reads its basis from the season of the date its page named, across a season's end too. The page is drawn again within the hour, so it takes the next season with no build. A date no season covers gets the season that began last: a city whose seasons run out keeps serving its latest day, and the start screen still lists it. Each of the six days is one open-ended season, from the date it was written for. Every season of a city keeps the start's words, weekday and first hour, which a page reads without the day's text, and the commit gate holds a closed season to holding on every date of its range. Kept days keep their own itinerary.

How long a day holds is worked out by code (`freshUntil` in `src/lib/days/written/freshness.ts`). The day is moved a week at a time to each date its page will show, and it holds while the light-sense check passes (`MIN_SHARE_IN_LIGHT_WINDOW`) and every stop begins in the light it began in on the date it was written for (`MAX_STOPS_IN_OTHER_LIGHT` is none). Light is read as the time axis draws it: day, golden hour or dark. `pnpm days:fresh` prints each city's shown date, its season, the last date its day holds and the days left, and flags and fails on a city with fewer than 21.

**Why.** Code computes each date's sun ("The model describes light; code computes it"), so what goes stale is the plan's fit to the light and the words written for it. A day written when its tile is pressed would take about 45 seconds instead of a tenth of one, reach a visitor unread, and break the page's static day and its version. A season in a file is read before it ships, plays as a written day does, and comes in on its date. The alarm reads the clock, so it runs beside the weekly production report and stays out of the commit gate, where a test that reads the clock would start failing every commit on a date; `freshUntil` itself is pure and tested on fixed dates. Soft and clear daylight count as one light because the axis never draws them apart and no day's words do: counted apart, Utrecht's noon stop changed light a week after its day was written. No stop may change, because the first to change in four of the six days is an evening stop whose words say it is dark or blue ("Dark by now", "the square turns blue") and which falls in golden hour from late January or February. The ranges are open-ended because the season that began last answers every later date anyway, and a `through` copied from today's `freshUntil` would go stale whenever the rule changes.

**Cost.** Read on 11 November 2026, the days hold until 5 December (Kanazawa), between 10 January and 6 February (Porto from the 22nd, Bologna, Utrecht, Madrid, Nafplio). Kanazawa's end is an alarm its words do not need: its garden at half past seven, whose words speak of low sun, is in golden hour from mid-December. Allowing one stop to change, as the light-sense check allows one miss, would hold the days until mid-March to mid-June and miss the evenings that turn golden. Porto fails on its shown date of 15 November, two of its five light windows met for 19 and 16 minutes against the 20 the check asks, and holds again from the 22nd. No check reads words that pin an hour or a season ("the low November sun", "the ground goes blue at around six"), so a day can count as holding while a line of it does not. A new season is a paid run of the model and ships with a release. A city's seasons share its start's first hour, so a season that starts later in winter moves the start's hour with it. The place checks are a season's, so a new season brings its run's checks with it.
